# Cutting electricity costs by choosing when to consume

## Summary of findings

- **Moving 1 MW of consumption out of each day's most expensive hour saves about €23,200 a year.** Four hours a day save €72,300, about 3.1 times as much, not four.
- **Since prices moved to quarter-hours in October 2025, acting in quarter-hours adds 19%** at one hour a day, €27,600, and 10% at four, €79,300. Measured hour by hour, the daily price gap has stayed around €47 to €53 per MWh since 2023.
- **Negative prices are rising every year**, from 0.80% of hours in 2021 to 7.70% in 2026, and they arrive at midday: 15.71% of all 13:00 hours are negative.
- **Price spikes are an evening event, and outside the 2021-22 gas crisis they are rare**: 0.54% to 1.13% of hours a year since 2023, against 46.29% in 2022.
- **The days with spikes are disproportionately the days of peak demand and of hardest gas-and-coal ramping.** If demand had nothing to do with spikes, an overlap this large would occur by chance about once in 32,000 tries; for gas-and-coal ramping, once in 3,000.

## Summary of recommendations

- **Value flexible load at about €23,000 per MW per year for the first hour it can move each day**, and invest where the equipment's yearly cost per MW sits below the figure for the hours the process can really move.
- **Schedule each day against the next day's published prices, not a fixed timetable.** A timetable that always avoids the evening hours that used to be dearest earns about a third less, €15,300 per MW per year against €23,200.
- **Control in quarter-hours where the equipment allows**: €4,400 per MW per year more at one hour a day.
- **Put flexible capacity to work on weekdays and from March to June**, where the daily gap is widest. A weekend day offers about two thirds of a weekday, and December to February about half of March to June.
- **Build the business case on post-crisis prices, and recheck it once a full year of quarter-hour prices exists, after September 2026.** 2022's daily gap was nearly double any year since, and the quarter-hour figures rest on eleven months.

## Approach

The client is an industrial consumer or battery operator buying at the Dutch day-ahead price, asking when prices go negative or extreme, how predictable that is, and what to do differently. The data are day-ahead prices, actual demand and generation by production type for the Netherlands from the ENTSO-E Transparency Platform, 1 January 2021 to 31 August 2026. Prices are hourly until 30 September 2025 and quarter-hourly from 1 October 2025, when the European day-ahead market moved to 15-minute units.

- **A negative hour** is any hour with a price below zero in any of its market time units. **An extreme hour** has an average price above €225 per MWh, the 90th percentile of the whole range, fixed once so every year is measured against the same bar.
- **The saving from moving load** is each day's most expensive price minus its median price, per MWh moved: the consumer escapes the dearest unit and lands in an ordinary one. Yearly values are the average daily saving times 365.
- **Whether spikes coincide with demand or with gas-and-coal ramping** is tested month by month: the days with a spike are set against the same number of days with the highest demand, or the largest swing in gas-and-coal output, and the chance of the overlap is computed exactly while holding the day of the week fixed, since spikes and high demand both avoid weekends.

Every figure below is regenerated by the file in `analysis/` named beside it.

## Findings

- **What moving load is worth.**
  The first hour escaped is worth €63.65 per MWh on average, and each further hour escapes a cheaper one than the last, which is why the table's columns grow more slowly than the hours. The figures assume the consumer knows each day's prices in advance, which their publication the afternoon before allows: a fixed timetable avoiding 18:00 to 21:00, the hours that were dearest before October 2025, earns €15,300 for one hour a day. Letting moved load land in the next day changes the figures by €100 to €400. (`analysis/04_shifting_gain.sql`; the timetable and next-day figures as of commit `91633ed`.)

| Moved in | € per MWh, 1 hour a day | € per MW per year, 1 hour a day | 2 hours | 4 hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| hours | 63.65 | 23,200 | 43,600 | 72,300 |
| quarter-hours | 75.58 | 27,600 | 49,000 | 79,300 |

- **What the move to quarter-hours changed.**
  Measured on the same days, the dearest quarter-hour of a day sits €7.0 to €36.4 per MWh further above the day's ordinary price than the dearest hour does, in every month and on every day of the week. Measured hour by hour, the typical daily gap was €33.4 per MWh in 2021 and €92.9 in 2022, then €47.2, €50.9, €52.7 and €48.6 from 2023 to 2026, so the market's own gap has not grown since the crisis. Month by month there is no step at October 2025: five of the eleven months since the switch fall within the range the same month took from 2023 to 2025, October to December and February fall below it, March and June above it, and the same month moves this much between years anyway, June running €39, €66, €65 and €91. A consumer that can only act hour by hour has not seen the opportunity grow since 2023; one that can act by the quarter-hour has. (`analysis/04_shifting_gain.sql`)

- **When the gap is widest.**
  Since the switch, at quarter-hour grain, the typical daily gap is €88.6 per MWh on Mondays and €60.5 to €72.2 on the other weekdays, against €47.6 on Saturdays and €45.7 on Sundays. By month it is €89.8 to €104.1 from March to June, except May at €63.5, against €30.2 to €46.6 from December to February. (`analysis/04_shifting_gain.sql`)

- **When prices go negative.**
  Negative hours were 0.80% of all hours in 2021, then 0.97%, 3.61%, 5.21%, 6.76%, and 7.70% in 2026 so far. They peak at 13:00 and vanish by 20:00; they are 8.71% of hours in May against 0.12% in February, and 9.88% on Sundays against 2.29% on Wednesdays. The sharpest point is 14:00 in May, negative in 38.17% of hours. Between 10:00 and 17:00 the worst 5% of hours pay the consumer to take electricity, by up to €21.58 per MWh at 14:00. In negative hours the share of output that cannot be attributed to a fuel rises by 42 percentage points and gas falls by 23. This source cannot say how much of that unattributed output is solar, so it cannot attribute the rise in negative hours to any one technology. (`analysis/01_negative_and_extreme.sql`, `analysis/02_generation_mix.sql`)

- **When prices spike.**
  Extreme hours peak at 19:00 and are fewest at 13:00, the mirror of the negative hours, and Wednesday has 11.69% against Sunday's 6.00%. Of the 4,956 extreme hours in the range, 4,055 fell in 2022; since 2023 they have been 0.54% to 1.13% of hours a year. Even at 19:00, 95% of hours never dip below €64.94 per MWh. In extreme hours gas's share of generation rises by 12 percentage points and coal's by 8. (`analysis/01_negative_and_extreme.sql`, `analysis/02_generation_mix.sql`)

- **What spike days have in common.**
  Of the 364 spike days that can be tested this way, 250 were also among their month's top-demand days, against 233 expected by chance, and 242 among its top gas-and-coal ramping days, against 226. This is good evidence of an association between extreme days, not proof of a cause, and not a correlation across ordinary days. For a consumer it means the expensive days are the ones on which everyone else also needs power, so flexibility pays most when the system is tightest. Most of the evidence comes from 2022, when spike days were common; from 2023 the yearly tests rest on 20 to 35 spike days each, and only 2023's demand result clears chance on its own. The unattributed output behaves the other way: days on which its daily minimum stays high are spike days less often than chance would give, about once in 600 tries. (`analysis/03_swing.sql`)

## Recommendation

- **Valuing the equipment.** Read the table above at the row for how the process can move, in hours or quarter-hours, and the column for how many hours a day it can move: that is the yearly saving per MW its annualised cost has to sit under. A process that can move two hours a day in quarter-hour steps is worth about €49,000 per MW per year.
- **Scheduling.** The daily shape is regular enough to plan around, midday cheap and evening dear, but the size of the gap varies widely from year to year. Next-day prices are published each afternoon, so the schedule can be set from them without forecasting anything; a fixed timetable is the fallback for a process that needs more notice than that.

## Caveats

- **Eleven months of quarter-hour prices**, one year after the switch against two or three before it, in a market where the same month varies by up to €52 per MWh between years.
- **Wholesale price only.** Network charges, taxes and supplier margins are left out, on the assumption that they do not vary by hour. A contract with time-of-use tariffs changes the figures.
- **A consumer too small to move the price.** An operator shifting enough load to set the price would narrow the gap it exploits.
- **Moved load lands at an ordinary price**, the day's median, not its cheapest unit, since a sizeable shift cannot all fit there. That understates the saving for small volumes.
- **The hours moved, 1, 2 and 4, are illustrative.** The value of each extra hour falls to about zero by twelve, where the dearest remaining hour reaches the day's median.
- **What this source cannot see.** Output that cannot be attributed to a fuel, including most solar, is published as one bucket or not at all, so the renewable share cannot be measured, and gas and coal output stands in for it. Grid congestion, part of why flexibility is worth money to this client, is not measured either, so no value it adds is in these figures.
