## Q1_NEG_EXT — how often prices are negative or extreme, and when

Regenerated by `analysis/01_negative_and_extreme.sql`.

**Totals.** 1,970 negative hours, 3.97% of the range. 4,956 extreme hours, 9.98%. No hour is both.

**By hour of day.** Negatives peak at 13:00 at 15.71% of that hour's hours and fall to zero at 20:00. Extremes are the mirror: they peak at 19:00 at 17.25% and bottom at 13:00. The two are inverse across the whole day, not merely different.

**How deep, by hour of day.** The 5th percentile of the deepest quarter-hour crosses zero at 10:00, bottoms at 14:00 at −21.58 EUR/MWh and is back above zero by 17:00 — a seven-hour window in which the worst 5% of hours pay the consumer. Outside it the same statistic reaches +64.94 at 19:00: even the cheapest moments of the evening are expensive.

**By day of week.** Sunday 9.88% negative against 6.00% extreme; Wednesday 2.29% against 11.69%. The 5th percentile of depth splits the week at zero: Sunday −4.39, Saturday −0.23, every weekday between +7.73 and +21.87.

**By month.** Negatives peak in May at 8.71% and are near absent in February, 0.12%. Extremes appear to peak in December at 23.79% — but 844 of December's 885 extreme hours are in 2021-2022, and with those years removed every month falls between 0.10% and 1.84%. The monthly extreme profile is when the crisis happened, not what months do.

**By year.** Negatives rise monotonically as a share of hours: 0.80, 0.97, 3.61, 5.21, 6.76, 7.70%. Extremes do not follow: 7.32, 46.29, 0.54, 0.76, 0.91, 1.13%.

**By hour within month.** The sharpest cell is May at 14:00, 38.17% of its hours negative, with a *median* hourly price of 17.92 EUR/MWh. Seven of the top eight cells are months 4-6 crossed with hours 12-15.

**Author's reading.** The weekday/weekend split and the daily shape are consistent with consumer usage. The monthly shape was initially puzzling and resolved as an artefact of the crisis rather than a seasonal effect.

## Q2_MIX — how the generation mix shifts when prices go negative or extreme

Regenerated by `analysis/02_generation_mix.sql`.

**The shift over the whole range, in percentage points of median share.** In negative hours `B20` is +41.75 and `B04` is −22.76; `B05` −11.73, `B19` +1.42, `B16` +1.00, `B18` +0.24. In extreme hours the two largest reverse: `B04` +12.30, `B05` +7.95, `B20` −5.20, `B18` −5.16, `B19` −2.09.

**Grouped by year, three things appear that the pooled figure hides.**

*`B20`'s negative-hour shift grows.* 32.0, 35.2, 28.1, 40.2, 46.4, 48.7 from 2021 to 2026. The pooled +41.75 is an average over a trend. `B04` shows no trend across the same years, sitting between −16.9 and −23.3.

*The extreme-hour shift is understated by pooling, not overstated.* `B04` reads 10.0 and 8.7 in 2021-2022 but 26.7, 25.9, 27.5 and 22.3 from 2023. In 2022 nearly half of all hours were extreme, so "extreme hours" and "all hours" were close to the same population and the contrast collapsed. Separating the years roughly doubles it.

*Wind changes sign.* `B18` offshore runs +2.6 in 2021 to −2.5 and −3.8 in 2025-2026 on the negative side; `B19` onshore falls from +7.0 to +0.6. The pooled figures, +0.24 and +1.42, show neither.

**Who leads, by month.** `B04` leads eight months of twelve; `B20` leads March, April and May. Four of `B04`'s eight leads are inside 2.24 points — October 1.06, March 1.17 the other way, July 1.65, February 2.24 — against January 9.42 and November 8.94. So the two are close for about half the year and gas pulls away only in November to January.

**Third place.** `B05` holds third every month except May and June, where `B18` takes it at roughly half the share. The gap between second and third widens from 6-13 points to 23.91 and 25.99 in those two months.

**`B20` is not interpretable as a share**, and that is a result rather than a gap. Its share peaks in April-May at 33%, dips in August to 24.6% and is lowest in November at 22.0% — a curve matching neither solar nor heating. The two measurables that split it by behaviour do work: the daily swing runs 2,956 MW in January to 8,262 in June, and the daily floor runs 2,372 in February to 1,034 in August. They peak in opposite seasons.

**`B20` over time.** The swing more than doubles, 3,861 MW in 2021 to 8,062 in 2026; the floor steps once between 2022 and 2023, 1,439 to 1,980, and is flat afterwards. 2026 covers 243 days ending 31 August, so its swing excludes the four lowest months and is biased upward.

**Author's reading.** The shift is more pronounced on the negative side than the extreme side, for fossils and `B20` alike. `B20` remains hard to interpret directly.

## Q3_SWING — whether the days of extreme price are the days of extreme load, thermal ramping and B20 behaviour

Regenerated by `analysis/03_swing.sql`. **Written by the agent at the author's instruction on 2026-10-04 and revised 2026-10-05**, unlike Q1 and Q2. Thermal output and the `B20` daily floor were a separate question until 2026-10-05 and are reported here.

**The rule.** Within one month of one year, n is that group's count of extreme-price days — days holding at least one hour above 225.00 EUR/MWh. The n days of largest value are then taken from the same group by four series — daily peak load, daily thermal swing, `B20` daily swing and `B20` daily floor — and the group reports how many of the n coincide. Thermal output is `B04` gas plus `B05` coal per hour, standing in for the renewable share this source cannot measure; its daily swing is the day's `max − min`, so its top days are the ones the burned fleet ramped hardest. The `B20` floor is a level rather than a swing, so its top days are the ones the unidentifiable bucket never fell low. Price sizes the set, so no threshold is chosen for any of the four.

**The groups.** 68 month-years, holding 487 extreme days. 21 groups contain none. 4 are extreme on every day of the month — March, July, August and September 2022 — where the top n of any series is the whole month and the match carries nothing; they take 123 of the 487 days with them. The 43 remaining groups hold 364 extreme days and carry the answer.

**The test, and the one null model behind every probability.** If a series had nothing to do with price, which of a month's Mondays carry the extreme flag would be unrelated to which of its Mondays are in the top n, and likewise for every weekday. Inside one weekday of one month the overlap is then hypergeometric, P(K = j) = C(E,j)·C(D−E, T−j)/C(D,T), with D days, E of them extreme and T in the top n. A month's overlap is the sum of its seven weekday overlaps, so its distribution is their convolution, and a year's or the whole range's is the convolution of their months. The p-value is the probability that luck alone does at least as well as the series did.

The weekday is held fixed because the calendar is a common cause: 28.6% of days are weekends but only 18.1% of extreme days and 9.1% of top-load days are, so the two sets overlap for calendar reasons alone. A null treating every day of the month alike credits that overlap to load, and earlier versions of this section did exactly that.

**Over the whole range.**

| Series | Matched | Expected by chance | SD | p, at least this many | p, at most this many |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Daily peak load | 250 of 364, 68.7% | 232.7, 63.9% | 4.13 | 0.000031 | 1.00 |
| Daily thermal swing | 242 of 364, 66.5% | 225.7, 62.0% | 4.56 | 0.00033 | 1.00 |
| `B20` daily swing | 224 of 364, 61.5% | 217.4, 59.7% | 4.17 | 0.074 | 0.95 |
| `B20` daily floor | 208 of 364, 57.1% | 220.8, 60.7% | 4.29 | 0.9993 | **0.0016** |

**Load and thermal ramping go with extreme price; the `B20` swing does not; the `B20` floor goes against it.** The chance that a series unrelated to price matches 250 or more is about 1 in 32,000; for thermal swing matching 242 or more, about 1 in 3,000. The `B20` swing's 7.4% is too common to rule out luck.

**The `B20` floor is the one series that avoids extreme days.** It matches 208, 2.98 standard deviations *below* chance, and the probability of doing that badly by luck is 0.0016. Within a month, the days on which the unidentifiable bucket stays high all day are less often extreme-price days than chance allows. The upper tail alone reports this as p = 0.9993, "no evidence of more", which is why the file carries both tails. What the bucket contains is unknown — that is what `B20` means — so the finding is about the bucket's behaviour, not about any technology in it.

**What the four together say about thermal output as the price-setter.** Of the two physical series that can be identified, thermal ramping matches the extreme days almost as well as demand does, and both clearly beat chance. That is consistent with extreme days being the days the burned fleet has to work hardest. It is measured through a substitute: gas and coal stand in for the renewable share, and some gas runs for heat rather than for price. For `B20` the chance of 224 or more is 7.4%, about 1 in 13 — not rare enough to rule out luck. The load effect is real and modest: 17 days above chance out of 364, roughly 5 percentage points.

**By year.**

| Year | Extreme days | Load matched / expected | p | Thermal matched / expected | p | `B20` swing matched / expected | p | `B20` floor matched / expected | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 79 | 63 / 63.3 | 0.74 | 66 / 62.1 | 0.015 | 64 / 62.1 | 0.16 | 62 / 63.2 | 0.87 |
| 2022 | 182 | 154 / 146.5 | 0.0003 | 154 / 144.0 | 0.0004 | 146 / 140.9 | 0.045 | 137 / 140.4 | 0.93 |
| 2023 | 22 | 12 / 6.2 | 0.0013 | 7 / 4.8 | 0.15 | 0 / 3.4 | 1.00 | 3 / 4.4 | 0.93 |
| 2024 | 20 | 2 / 3.6 | 0.93 | 1 / 3.7 | 0.99 | 2 / 1.4 | 0.46 | 4 / 3.5 | 0.50 |
| 2025 | 35 | 9 / 6.5 | 0.15 | 9 / 5.8 | 0.064 | 5 / 4.9 | 0.59 | 0 / 4.6 | 1.00 |
| 2026 | 26 | 10 / 6.5 | 0.046 | 5 / 5.3 | 0.68 | 7 / 4.7 | 0.12 | 2 / 4.7 | 0.98 |

The p columns are the upper tail. The floor's avoidance is spread across years rather than concentrated in one: it falls short of chance in five of six, and in 2025 it matches none of 35 extreme days against 4.6 expected.

Three cells are below 0.01: load in 2022 and 2023, and thermal swing in 2022. With six years tested per series, a p near 0.05 turns up by luck often enough that load's 0.046 in 2026, thermal's 0.015 in 2021 and `B20`'s 0.045 in 2022 are not evidence on their own. Thermal swing's evidence sits in the crisis years and none of 2023 to 2026 reaches 0.05; those years rest on 20 to 35 extreme days each, so the test has little room to show anything there. The plan expected thermal output to track price less well each year as more of what sets the price stops being burned. The data agree in shape — clear evidence in 2021 and 2022, none from 2023 — but the later years are too thin to establish a decline rather than an absence of power, so this is consistent with the expectation, not a demonstration of it.

**This corrects the previous reading of 2022.** Earlier versions said the crisis-year matches were almost entirely forced. In size they are — 2022 is 4 points above chance — but months where n is close to the whole month leave very little room for luck, so a small excess there is statistically solid. 2021 is the year that is genuinely nothing.

**By month.** Against the 2.15 that 43 tests at 0.05 would throw by luck, load clears it in 5 months, thermal swing in 5, the `B20` swing in 1 and the `B20` floor in none. Median p is 0.82 for load, 0.70 for thermal and 1.00 for both `B20` measures. Holding the weekday fixed leaves little randomness inside a small month — a month with one extreme day often has an expected overlap of exactly zero — so most months cannot show anything individually, and the evidence lives in the pooled and yearly figures.

**The non-intersections are listed as dates.** Two statements give them per group: the extreme-price days in none of the four series' top n, 25 groups' worth, and the top-load days that were not extreme at all, 39 groups' worth.

**Not changed, and it moves every number:** an extreme day is still any day with one or more extreme hours, so a day extreme for one hour counts the same as one extreme for 24.

**Author's reading.** Three series give results: daily peak load, daily thermal swing and the `B20` daily floor. Over the 364 testable extreme days they are good evidence, not proof, of an association between extreme-price days and the days of peak load and of hardest thermal ramping: if those series had nothing to do with price, an overlap this large would occur by chance about once in 32,000 tries for load and once in 3,000 for thermal swing. The `B20` floor carries evidence of the same strength in the opposite direction: days on which the unidentifiable bucket stays high are extreme-price days less often than chance would give, about once in 600 tries. The test asks only whether the most extreme days of each series coincide, so this is an association between extremes, not a correlation across all days. The `B20` swing, the by-month results and the by-year results rest on too little data to support a claim, except load in 2022 and 2023 and thermal swing in 2022, which clear chance in their own right.

## Q4_GAIN — what a consumer saves by choosing when to buy, and what the move to quarter-hours changed

Regenerated by `analysis/04_shifting_gain.sql`. **The before-and-after comparison was drafted by the author on 2026-10-05**; the agent corrected the measurable to `max − median` and the per-group statistic to the median, extended the author's side-by-side layout from the day of week to the month and the year, and moved the file onto `table_price_unit`. **The decomposition and the sensitivity were written by the agent the same day, at the author's instruction.**

**The measure.** The daily spread is the day's most expensive market time unit minus its median one: the saving, per MWh moved, of escaping the worst unit and landing in an ordinary one rather than the single cheapest. Each period is measured at its own grain — the day's 24 hourly prices through 2025-09-30, its 96 quarter-hour prices from 2025-10-01 — and each group reports the median of its daily spreads, since the 2021-2022 crisis drags every mean up. All figures in EUR/MWh.

It is everything that differs between the two periods — different years and price levels as well as the grain — and not the grain alone. ### The sensitivity: what this is worth per year

**From EUR per MWh to EUR per year, per MW of load the client can move**, over the same 335 days so both scenarios are priced identically. Two assumptions are varied: how many hours a day the MW is moved, and whether it moves in hours or quarter-hours. Held fixed: the client knows each day's prices in advance, as day-ahead publication allows; moved load lands within the same day at an ordinary, median unit rather than the cheapest; the client is too small to move the price; and only the wholesale price counts. Annual figures are the mean daily saving times 365, since a year is a total.

| Block | EUR/MWh, 1 h a day | EUR per MW per year, 1 h | 2 h | 4 h |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| hour | 63.65 | 23,200 | 43,600 | 72,300 |
| quarter | 75.58 | 27,600 | 49,000 | 79,300 |

**Reading it.**

- **Quarter-hour blocks add 19% at one hour a day**, 4,400 per MW per year, falling to 10% at four hours, 7,000. The finer grain matters most to a client moving little load, because its advantage is in the very top units.
- **Volume has diminishing returns.** Four hours a day earns 3.1 times one hour, not 4, since each further hour escaped is cheaper than the last.

The figure a client sets against the cost of the equipment is therefore about **23,000 to 80,000 EUR per MW of flexible load per year** over this period, decided mostly by how many hours they can move and less by the grain. It rests on eleven months of one regime, October 2025 to August 2026, and on the client acting on each day's prices.

**Two assumptions were tested and then held, to keep the study simple.** A client with no notice, following a fixed timetable that avoids the hours dearest before the switch, earned about a third less: 15,300 per MW per year for one hour a day against 23,200. Letting moved load land in the next day changed the figures by 100 to 400. Neither is computed by the file any longer; both figures are regenerated by `analysis/04_shifting_gain.sql` as it stood at commit `91633ed`.
