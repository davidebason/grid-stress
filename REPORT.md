# Cutting electricity costs by choosing when to consume

## Summary of findings

- **Negative prices grew more common in every full year, and are predictable: middays from March to September, and weekends.** They rose from 0.80% of all hours in 2021 to 6.76% in 2025; 2026, which our data covers only to August, is left out of the comparison. From March to September 18.15% of the hours between 10:00 and 17:00 are negative, and Sundays have four times the weekday rate.
- **Extreme prices have been rare since the 2021-22 gas crisis, and come on weekday evenings, most often in winter.** An **extreme hour** averages above €225 per MWh, a bar only the dearest tenth of all hours since 2021 clears. Nearly half of 2022's hours cleared it, 0.54% to 0.91% a year from 2023 to 2025, and 1.13% in 2026 to August: 260 hours since 2023, 192 of them between 16:00 and 22:00 and all but two on weekdays.
- **When prices go negative, gas and coal step back and output that cannot be attributed to a fuel rises; when they go extreme, gas and coal step up.** In negative hours the unattributed share of published generation rises by 42 percentage points and gas falls by 23; in extreme hours gas rises by 12 and coal by 8.
- **Extreme days, those with at least one extreme hour, are disproportionately the month's days of highest load and of widest gas-and-coal swing.** Of 364 extreme days, 250 were among their month's days of highest load, against 233 that chance would give, and 242 among its days of widest gas-and-coal swing, against 226. Chance alone produces an overlap as large as 250 about once in 32,000 tries, and one as large as 242 about once in 3,000.
- **Moving 1 MW of consumption out of each day's most expensive hour saves about €23,200 a year.** The saving per MWh moved is the daily **price gap**, a day's most expensive price minus its median price, which has averaged €63.65 per MWh since October 2025. Four hours a day save €72,300, about 3.1 times as much, not four.
- **Prices have been set per quarter-hour since October 2025, and acting on them saves more: €27,600 per MW a year at one hour a day, 19% above €23,200, and €79,300 at four hours, 10% above €72,300.** The extra comes from escaping the dearest fifteen minutes rather than the dearest hour, not from a more volatile market: measured hour by hour, the typical daily price gap, the median over a year's days, has stayed between €47 and €53 per MWh in every year since 2023, 2026 to August included.

## Summary of recommendations

- **Invest in flexibility only where it costs less per year than it saves.** Each MW of consumption moved out of the day's dearest hour saves about €23,000 a year, and moved for four hours about €72,000, more in quarter-hours (the table under "4. What would a consumer who could shift its consumption actually gain?" below). Equipment that makes a process flexible is worth installing when its **yearly cost per MW**, the purchase price spread over its working life plus the cost of running it, sits below the saving for the hours the process can really move.
- **Schedule each day against the next day's prices**, published the afternoon before, so the dearest hours can be avoided without forecasting them. Every saving here assumes it.
- **Control in quarter-hours where the equipment allows**: €4,400 per MW per year more at one hour a day.
- **Use flexible capacity most on weekdays and from March to June**, where the daily price gap is widest: a weekend day offers about two thirds of a weekday, and December to February about half of March to June.
- **When estimating what flexibility will save, use prices from 2023 onward, never an average that includes 2022.** In 2022, at the height of the gas crisis, the typical daily price gap was €92.9 per MWh, nearly double the €47 to €53 of every year since, 2026 to August included, so an estimate that includes it promises savings no later year has delivered. The savings in this memo are computed from the months since October 2025 only.
- **Recalculate the savings in the table under "4. What would a consumer who could shift its consumption actually gain?" below once September 2026's prices are published, completing the first full year of quarter-hour prices.** Those savings are averaged over the only quarter-hour months there are so far, October 2025 to August 2026: they contain no September, and each other month comes from a single year, although the same month's typical daily price gap has differed by up to €52 per MWh from one year to the next.

## Approach

The client is an industrial consumer or battery operator buying at the Dutch day-ahead price, asking when prices go negative or extreme, how predictable that is, and what to do differently. The data are day-ahead prices, actual **load**, the electricity used across the whole country, and generation by production type for the Netherlands from the ENTSO-E Transparency Platform, 1 January 2021 to 31 August 2026. Each price covers one **market time unit**: an hour until 30 September 2025, and a quarter-hour from 1 October 2025, when the European day-ahead market moved to 15-minute units.

- **A negative hour** is any hour with a price below zero in any of its market time units, and marks surplus: more electricity on offer than the country can use. **An extreme hour** has an average price above €225 per MWh, the 90th percentile of the whole range, which we fix once so that every year meets the same bar. It marks scarcity: load pressing against what the cheaper plants can supply, so that the most expensive ones, mostly gas, set the price. **An extreme day** is a day with at least one extreme hour.
- **Top days.** Within each calendar month of each year, the month's **top load days** are its days of highest peak load, as many as it has extreme days. Its **top gas-and-coal days** are chosen the same way by the day's **gas-and-coal swing**, its highest hourly gas-and-coal output minus its lowest, which measures how hard those plants ramped up and down. Top days for the output that cannot be attributed to a fuel are chosen the same way twice, by its daily swing and by its daily minimum.
- **The generation mix** is each source's share of the electricity published as generated in an hour. We measure how it moves as the median share in negative hours, or in extreme hours, minus the median share in all hours, in percentage points. The largest single entry for the Netherlands is output the grid operator cannot attribute to a fuel.
- **Whether extreme days coincide with top days.** In each calendar month of each year we count how many extreme days are also top days, and set that count beside the count chance would give. Separately, we compute exactly the probability that chance alone produces an overlap as far from chance's count as ours. Both hold the day of the week fixed, since extreme prices and high load both avoid weekends. A month with no extreme day, or one every day, cannot show an overlap beyond chance and is left out. We use gas and coal because the renewable share cannot be measured from this source, for the reason under "Caveats" below: what is burned is what remains once wind and sun have supplied what they can, so gas-and-coal output moves opposite to renewable output and serves as an **inverse indicator** of it.
- **The daily price gap** is a day's most expensive price minus its median price, in € per MWh, over its market time units: its hourly prices until 30 September 2025, its quarter-hourly ones from 1 October 2025. It is what a consumer saves per MWh by moving consumption out of the day's dearest unit into an ordinary one. Its **typical** value over a period is the median over that period's days; an average is called an average.
- **Savings.** Moving one hour a day saves the daily price gap on each MWh moved; each further hour escapes the next-dearest unit, so saves less. We take a year's saving per MW as the average daily saving times 365.

Every number below is regenerated by the file in `analysis/` named beside it.

## Findings

### 1. How often are prices negative or extreme, and when?

- **When prices go negative.**
  Negative hours were 0.80% of all hours in 2021, then 0.97%, 3.61%, 5.21% and 6.76% in 2025, rising in every full year. 2026 runs only to August, and those months hold most of a year's negative hours, so we compare it with the same months of earlier years: 7.70% against 8.18% for January to August 2025. From March to September, 18.15% of the hours between 10:00 and 17:00 are negative, against under 1% at other times of day, peaking at 38.17% at 14:00 in May. May is the peak month at 8.71%, ahead of July and August at 5.44%, and November to February stay below 1.6%. Sundays are negative in 9.88% of hours and Saturdays in 6.31%, against 2.13% to 2.52% on weekdays, as load falls while the sun still shines. From October to February, 144 of the 214 negative hours fall between midnight and 07:00. Between 10:00 and 17:00 the worst 5% of hours pay the consumer to take electricity, by up to €21.58 per MWh at 14:00. (`analysis/01_negative_and_extreme.sql`)
- **When prices go extreme.**
  4,055 of the 4,956 extreme hours fell in 2022, in the gas crisis. Of the 260 since 2023, 192 fell between 16:00 and 22:00, peaking at 19:00, 34 between 07:00 and 10:00, and none between midnight and 07:00; all but two fell on a weekday, and 142 from October to February against 7 in April and May. Even at 19:00, 95% of hours stay above €64.94 per MWh. For a consumer, the cheap hours are spring and summer middays and weekends, and the dear ones are weekday evenings, most of all in winter. (`analysis/01_negative_and_extreme.sql`)

### 2. What is on the system when it happens?

- **In negative hours.**
  Over the whole range, the unattributed share of published generation is 41.75 percentage points higher than in an ordinary hour, gas 22.76 lower and coal 11.73 lower; wind and the small identifiable solar fleet move by under 1.5 points. The unattributed rise grows over time: 28.1 points in 2023, then 40.2 and 46.4 in 2025, and 48.7 in 2026 to August. Part of that output follows the sun, swinging by 8,262 MW across a June day against 2,956 MW in January, but this source cannot say how much of it is solar, so the rise cannot be pinned on any one technology. The season decides what is on the system: from March to September the unattributed share rises by 34 to 45 points in negative hours, while from October to February, 214 negative hours in all, it rises by 2 to 12 and published offshore and onshore wind together by 14 to 28. Summer's negative prices are a sunny midday surplus, winter's a windy night. (`analysis/02_generation_mix.sql`)
- **In extreme hours.**
  Gas is 12.30 percentage points higher than in an ordinary hour and coal 7.95, while the unattributed output and offshore wind are each about 5 lower. Since 2023, 2026 to August included, gas is 22.3 to 27.5 points higher and offshore wind 7.4 to 10.7 lower: prices go extreme when the wind drops and gas fills the gap. The whole-range rise in gas is smaller because in 2022 nearly half of all hours were extreme, so extreme hours and ordinary hours were close to the same population. (`analysis/02_generation_mix.sql`)

### 3. On the days prices go extreme, are load and the burned fleet at their extremes too?

An extreme day is a day with at least one extreme hour. Each result gives two numbers: the overlap we count, beside the overlap chance would give, and the probability that chance alone produces an overlap as far from it as ours.

- **Load and the gas-and-coal swing, yes.**
  On extreme days, load and the gas-and-coal swing tend to be at their monthly highest too. Of the 364 extreme days we can test, 250 were top load days, against 233 that chance would give, and 242 were top gas-and-coal days, against 226. Chance alone produces an overlap as large as 250 about once in 32,000 tries, and one as large as 242 about once in 3,000. Each month is tested on its own, with the day of the week held fixed, so the overlap is not winter or the working week bringing both. This shows that the extreme days go together, not that one causes the other, and says nothing about ordinary days. For a consumer, the expensive days are the ones on which everyone else also needs power, so flexibility pays most when the system is tightest. Half the evidence comes from 2022, when extreme days were common: 182 of the 364. Since 2023 each year has only 20 to 35 extreme days, and chance alone produces most years' overlaps at least once in 7 tries; the exceptions are load in 2023, about once in 800, and in 2026 to August, about once in 22, and the gas-and-coal swing in 2025, about once in 16. (`analysis/03_swing.sql`)
- **The unattributed output: its daily minimum, yes, in the opposite direction to load; its daily swing, no.**
  Of the 364 extreme days, 208 were among the days on which its daily minimum stayed highest, against 221 that chance would give: fewer, where load gave more. Chance alone produces an overlap as small as 208 about once in 600 tries, so this is not chance either: extreme days tend not to be the days on which the unattributed output stays high all day. By its daily swing the overlap is 224, against 217, which chance alone produces about once in 13 tries, so there is no association either way. (`analysis/03_swing.sql`)

### 4. What would a consumer who could shift its consumption actually gain?

- **What moving consumption is worth.**
  The first hour escaped is worth €63.65 per MWh on average, and each further hour escapes a cheaper one than the last, which is why the table's columns grow more slowly than the hours. (`analysis/04_shifting_gain.sql`)

| Moved in | € per MWh, 1 hour a day | € per MW per year, 1 hour a day | 2 hours | 4 hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| hours | 63.65 | 23,200 | 43,600 | 72,300 |
| quarter-hours | 75.58 | 27,600 | 49,000 | 79,300 |

- **What the move to quarter-hours changed.**
  On the same days, the dearest quarter-hour of a day sits €7.0 to €36.4 per MWh further above the day's median price than the dearest hour does, in every month and on every day of the week. Measured hour by hour, the typical daily price gap was €33.4 per MWh in 2021 and €92.9 in 2022, then €47.2, €50.9 and €52.7 from 2023 to 2025, and €48.6 in 2026 to August: the market's own gap has not grown since the crisis. Nor is there a step at October 2025. Five of the eleven months since fall within the range the same month took from 2023 to 2025, October to December and February below it, March and June above it, and the same month moves this much between years anyway, June running €39, €66, €65 and €91. Acting hour by hour, the opportunity has not grown since 2023; acting by the quarter-hour, it has. (`analysis/04_shifting_gain.sql`)
- **When the gap is widest, and where in the day the dearest unit sits.**
  Since the switch, at quarter-hour grain, the typical daily price gap is €88.6 per MWh on Mondays and €60.5 to €72.2 on the other weekdays, against €47.6 on Saturdays and €45.7 on Sundays; €89.8 to €104.1 from March to June, except May at €63.5, against €30.2 to €46.6 from December to February. The day's dearest quarter-hour fell between 16:00 and 22:00 on 72% of days, and between 06:00 and 10:00 on 17%. (`analysis/04_shifting_gain.sql`)

## Recommendation

- **Valuing the equipment.** The table above gives the yearly saving per MW that the equipment's yearly cost per MW has to sit under: the row for whether the process moves in hours or quarter-hours, the column for how many hours a day. A process that can move two hours a day in quarter-hour steps is worth about €49,000 per MW per year.
- **Scheduling.** The daily shape, midday cheap and evening dear, is regular enough to plan around, but the size of the gap varies widely from year to year. A process that needs more notice than a day ahead would capture less than these savings.

## Caveats

- **Eleven months of quarter-hour prices**, against two or three years of the same months before the switch, in a market where the same month varies by up to €52 per MWh between years.
- **Wholesale price only.** We leave out network charges, taxes and supplier margins, assuming they do not vary by hour; a contract with time-of-use tariffs changes the savings.
- **A consumer too small to move the price.** An operator shifting enough consumption to set the price would narrow the gap it exploits.
- **Moved consumption lands at an ordinary price**, the day's median, not its cheapest unit, since a sizeable shift cannot all fit there, which understates the saving for small volumes.
- **The hours moved, 1, 2 and 4, are illustrative.** The value of each extra hour falls to about zero by twelve, where the dearest remaining hour reaches the day's median.
- **What this source cannot see.** Wind and solar appear in the data, but only the output the grid operator can attribute to them, from plant connected to its transmission grid. Published solar stays at 47 to 67 MW of mean output in every year from 2021 to 2025 and in 2026 to August, while installed Dutch solar capacity more than doubled, from 11.1 GW of panel capacity at the end of 2020 to 28.6 GW at the end of 2024, according to Statistics Netherlands (CBS), [*Hernieuwbare energie in Nederland 2024*](https://www.cbs.nl/nl-nl/longread/rapportages/2025/hernieuwbare-energie-in-nederland-2024/5-zonne-energie), table 5.1.1; the rest is filed as unattributed output or not published at all (`evidence/04_generation_identifiability.sql`). So the renewable share cannot be measured, and the gas-and-coal indicator used in its place is imperfect: imports, nuclear and gas burned for heat move it too. Grid congestion, part of why flexibility is worth money to this client, is not measured either, so no value it adds is in these savings.
